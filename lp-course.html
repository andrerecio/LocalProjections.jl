Variance decompositions and joint inference

Plagborg-Møller’s local-projection course: temperature shocks and world GDP (Bilal and Känzig), 1960–2019

Mikkel Plagborg-Møller’s short course Local projections for applied macroeconomics (2026) works one example through its Stata code: the effect of global temperature shocks on world GDP, after Bilal and Känzig (2026). This page reruns two of its steps that the package did not have: the variance decomposition of lecture 1, which asks how much of the GDP forecast error the temperature shock explains, and the joint inference of lecture 3, a Wald test of all horizons at once and a simultaneous confidence band. In Stata they take a loop of regress/predict, suest and a user-written command; here they are implicitfevd, jointtest and bands = :supt.

Data and specification

The course’s extract of the Bilal–Känzig data ships as docs/src/data/bilal_kanzig.csv: annual, 1960–2019, with world real GDP per capita (100·log, lnrgdppc_world_pwt), the global temperature anomaly (gtmp_bkly_aw_dtfe2s, the Hamilton-filter forecast error of global temperature in °C), a global-recession indicator, the log oil price and the one-year Treasury yield.

With \(y_t\) log GDP, \(x_t\) the anomaly, \(r_t\) the recession indicator and \(w_t\) the four lagged series, the local projection is

\[ y_{t+h} = \mu_h + \theta_h x_t + \sum_{j=0}^{2} \gamma_{h,j} r_{t-j} + \sum_{j=1}^{2} \delta_{h,j}' w_{t-j} + \xi_{h,t+h}, \qquad h = 0, \dots, 10, \]

with heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors, the course’s reg ..., robust. This is the argument of Montiel Olea and Plagborg-Møller (2021): once the projection controls for lags of the data (lag augmentation), the conventional heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors are valid, with no HAC correction and no bandwidth to choose. A recent version of the LP–VAR primer of Montiel Olea, Plagborg-Møller, Qian and Wolf (2025) goes one step further:

The LP estimator should be bias-corrected using the procedure of Herbst and Johannsen (2024). Instead of reporting HAC standard errors for LP, simply report the conventional heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors—or even better, the bootstrap confidence interval discussed in Supplemental Appendix B.

The package has both: biascorrect(m) for the Herbst–Johannsen correction and varbootstrap(m, data; vars, nlags) for that bootstrap. This page stays with the course’s HC1 standard errors.

m = lp(@formula(leads(lnrgdppc_world_pwt) ~ gtmp_bkly_aw_dtfe2s + recessiondates +
                lags(recessiondates, 2) + lags(lnrgdppc_world_pwt, 2) +
                lags(gtmp_bkly_aw_dtfe2s, 2) + lags(lnpoil_wti, 2) + lags(treasury1y, 2)),
       bk; horizon = 10)
summarize(m; level = 0.68)

The implicit shock

By Frisch–Waugh, \(\theta_h\) is the response to \(\tilde x_t\), the residual of \(x_t\) on all the controls: the movement in temperature that recessions and past economic conditions do not explain. implicitshock returns it, aligned with the rows of the data:

x̃ = implicitshock(m)
std(filter(isfinite, x̃))
0.10029931207558151

The implicit temperature shock (°C), the residual of the anomaly on the controls.

Its standard deviation, 0.10 °C, is the size of a typical shock, so a 1 °C impulse is about 10 standard deviations, an unusually large one.

Variance decomposition

How much of the uncertainty about future GDP is due to temperature shocks? The course’s recipe, a version of Gorodnichenko and Lee (2020), runs at each horizon two regressions:

  1. regress \(y_{t+h}\) on the controls (everything but \(x_t\)) and keep the forecast error \(\hat f_{h,t+h}\);
  2. regress \(\hat f_{h,t+h}\) on a constant and \(\tilde x_t, \tilde x_{t+1}, \dots, \tilde x_{t+h}\); the share is its \(R^2\).

The share is the fraction of the forecast-error variance that would disappear if the current and future shocks were known. implicitfevd runs both steps from the fitted projection:

fv = implicitfevd(m)

Temperature shocks explain 8.5% of the variance of the two-year GDP forecast error and 49.2% of the seven-year one, the figures on the course’s slides.

The package’s fevd, the port of Gorodnichenko and Lee’s own code, would not accept this specification as it stands: it treats the shock as observed and demeaned rather than residualized, conditions only on lagged variables, and needs a cumul or ldiff response. implicitfevd follows the course instead: the shock is \(\tilde x_t\), the forecast error conditions on the contemporaneous recession indicator too, and the \(R^2\) is the centred one of a regression with a constant. It has no standard errors, as in the course.

Joint inference across horizons

The 68% band above covers each horizon with probability 0.68 separately. A hypothesis about several horizons needs the covariance of the estimates across horizons, which are correlated because the regressions share their observations. jointvcov stacks the scores of every horizon on one time axis (Stata’s suest), and jointtest is the Wald test that the response is zero at every horizon:

t = jointtest(m)
Joint Wald test across horizons
  Term:       gtmp_bkly_aw_dtfe2s
  H₀:         θ_h = 0 for h ∈ {0:10}
  Vcov:       CovarianceMatrices.HR1()
  chi2(11) = 23.1552
  p-value:    0.0168

The estimates are strongly correlated across neighbouring horizons (the correlation of \(\hat\theta_5\) and \(\hat\theta_6\) is 0.85), so the pointwise band cannot be read as a joint statement. The null of no effect at any horizon is rejected with a p-value of 0.017.

A simultaneous band covers the whole response with probability 0.68. The sup-t band of Montiel Olea and Plagborg-Møller (2019) widens the pointwise band by a common critical value, the 68% quantile of \(\max_h |\hat\theta_h - \theta_h| / \mathrm{se}_h\), simulated from that correlation:

c68 = supt_critical_value(m; level = 0.68)
1.8771803288591733

Response of world GDP per capita (%) to a 1 °C temperature shock: 68% pointwise band (shaded) and 68% sup-t simultaneous band (dashed).

The simultaneous band uses 1.88 standard errors instead of one. It still excludes zero at horizons 4–8, so the negative effect of temperature shocks on GDP survives the joint reading. At the 90% level (critical value 2.42) it excludes zero at horizons 5–6. Any shape restriction can be checked the same way: a monotone response, for instance, is rejected if no monotone function fits inside the band.

bands = :supt works in summarize, plot and irfplot alike:

summarize(m; level = 0.68, bands = :supt)
plot(m; levels = [0.68], bands = :supt)

With an EWC(B) estimator the test becomes the fixed-smoothing \(F_{m,\,B-m+1}\) test and the sup-t draws come from the fixed-\(b\) limit, whose margins are Student-\(t_B\). With \(T = 58\) the LLSW rule gives only 6 cosine terms, fewer than the eleven horizons, and an EWC variance has rank at most \(B\), so the joint test is available only for a few horizons at a time:

jointtest(m, EWC(ewc_bandwidth(m)); horizons = 4:8)
