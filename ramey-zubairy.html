Government spending multipliers

Ramey and Zubairy (2018), linear model: US quarterly data, 1889Q1–2015Q4

Ramey and Zubairy (2018, JPE) estimate how much GDP rises when government spending rises, using more than a century of US quarterly data. This page replicates the baseline linear model from their Stata program jordagk.do: the impulse responses, the one-step cumulative multiplier, the two-step multiplier and the weak-instrument diagnostic. The authors build every lead, cumulated sum and lag by hand and loop ivreg2 over horizons. Here each is a single call.

Data and specification

The cleaned dataset ships with the package as docs/src/data/ramey_zubairy.csv, built from the replication package with the variable definitions of jordagk.do. GDP and spending are divided by potential GDP (the Gordon–Krenn normalization), so regression coefficients are already in dollars of GDP per dollar of spending.

rz = rz_data()

Shock Instrument \(s_t\) Controls \(w_{t-1}\)

newsy starts in 1890Q1: the file begins in 1889Q1, and its first lag does not exist.

Impulse responses

The responses of spending and GDP at horizon \(h\) are the coefficients on the shock in \(x_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h s_t + \gamma_h' w_{t-1} + u_{t+h}\). leads(x) puts \(x_{t+h}\) on the left and lp runs the regression for every \(h\) up to horizon:

irf_y_news = lp(@formula(leads(y) ~ newsy + lags(newsy, 4) + lags(y, 4) + lags(g, 4)),
    rz; horizon = 20)
irf_g_news = lp(@formula(leads(g) ~ newsy + lags(newsy, 4) + lags(y, 4) + lags(g, 4)),
    rz; horizon = 20)
irf_y_bp = lp(@formula(leads(y) ~ bp + lags(y, 4) + lags(g, 4)), rz; horizon = 20)
irf_g_bp = lp(@formula(leads(g) ~ bp + lags(y, 4) + lags(g, 4)), rz; horizon = 20)

hac = Bartlett{NeweyWest}()      # Newey–West, automatic bandwidth
summarize(irf_y_news, hac; term = :newsy, level = 0.90)

After a news shock both responses are hump-shaped: spending peaks at 0.37 after 11 quarters, GDP at 0.29 after 10. Under Blanchard–Perotti the shock is current spending, so spending moves one for one on impact, keeps rising to 2.25 after 3 quarters, then declines.

The one-step cumulative multiplier

The multiplier at horizon \(h\) is cumulative extra GDP per cumulative extra dollar of spending. Ramey and Zubairy estimate it in one step, as \(m_h\) in

\[ \sum_{j=0}^{h} y_{t+j} = \alpha_h + m_h \sum_{j=0}^{h} g_{t+j} + \gamma_h' w_{t-1} + u_{t+h}, \]

with cumulative spending instrumented by the shock \(s_t\). Both sides move with the horizon, and cumul handles either side, so lpiv returns the whole multiplier path at once:

mult_news = lpiv(TSLS(), @formula(cumul(y) ~ (cumul(g) ~ newsy) +
                                  lags(newsy, 4) + lags(y, 4) + lags(g, 4)), rz; horizon = 20)
mult_bp = lpiv(TSLS(), @formula(cumul(y) ~ (cumul(g) ~ bp) + lags(y, 4) + lags(g, 4)),
    rz; horizon = 20)

s_news = summarize(mult_news, hac; level = 0.90)   # the multiplier is `shock`, no `term`
s_bp = summarize(mult_bp, hac; level = 0.90)
nobs(mult_bp)[[1, 9, 21]]                          # h = 0, 8, 20: Stata's samples

At these 13 horizons, for both shocks, every point estimate is within 0.19 published standard errors of the authors’ value, with a median gap of 0.03. The remaining difference is most likely the data vintage: the spreadsheet of published numbers predates the last revision of the shipped data by a week.

The standard errors differ more, 0.77 to 1.14 times the published ones, for two reasons:

  • Bandwidth. The paper uses ivreg2, robust bw(auto). Both sides implement the Newey–West (1994) automatic rule, but they select different bandwidths on the same regression. With a fixed bandwidth, Bartlett(b) here and bw(b) in Stata, coefficients and IV standard errors agree to about \(10^{-10}\).
  • Degrees of freedom. ivreg2 applies no small-sample factor. The standard errors here, IV and OLS alike, carry \(n/(n-k)\) and are 1–3% larger than Stata’s ivreg2 equivalents.

The two-step multiplier

The paper’s alternative estimator is the ratio of the cumulative sums of the two impulse responses, \(\sum_{j \le h} \hat\beta^y_j \,/\, \sum_{j \le h} \hat\beta^g_j\):

twostep(ry, rg, shock) = cumsum(coefpath(ry; term = shock)) ./
                         cumsum(coefpath(rg; term = shock))
two_news = twostep(irf_y_news, irf_g_news, :newsy)
two_bp = twostep(irf_y_bp, irf_g_bp, :bp)
(news = maximum(abs.(two_news .- s_news.coef)), bp = maximum(abs.(two_bp .- s_bp.coef)))
(news = 0.0031353020818054578, bp = 0.0003613935999802753)

The two estimators agree to within 0.0031 (news) and 0.0004 (Blanchard–Perotti) at every horizon, because here the one-step regressions use the same sample as the impulse-response regressions. Ramey and Zubairy note that the two can diverge in other specifications, precisely when the samples differ.

Is the instrument strong enough?

A multiplier estimated with a weak instrument is biased and its bands undercover. weakivtest computes the Montiel Olea–Pflueger effective F at each horizon, from the HAC covariance attached to the model:

w_news = weakivtest(mult_news + vcov(hac))         # one test per horizon
w_bp = weakivtest(mult_bp + vcov(hac))
w_news[9]                                          # two years out

The news instrument never clears the critical value of 23.11: its effective F peaks at 19.57 at 5 quarters. Blanchard–Perotti is strong at short horizons and falls below the threshold from horizon 11 on. At \(h = 0\) its instrument is the regressor: the regression treats it as exogenous, there is no first stage to test (weakivtest returns nothing), and the figure omits that point. Ramey and Zubairy respond to weak instruments with Anderson–Rubin inference, which the package does not implement yet.

Bias correction and bootstrap bands

The bands above use Newey–West standard errors. Montiel Olea, Plagborg-Møller, Qian and Wolf (2025) recommend a different recipe for local projections: correct the estimates for small-sample bias with the procedure of Herbst and Johannsen (2024), and report either heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors or, better, the bands of a VAR moving-block bootstrap. The bootstrap fits a VAR to the data, its slopes corrected for bias with Pope’s (1990) formula, resamples its residuals in overlapping blocks, re-estimates the complete local projection on each artificial sample, and builds Hall percentile-\(t\) bands. biascorrect and varbootstrap implement it, with both corrections on by default. The procedure is for OLS projections, so it covers the impulse responses but not the IV multiplier.

The VAR is fitted on the shock and the two responses, which must have no missing values, so the first quarters, before newsy starts, are dropped. The estimation samples do not change. The reference picks the lag order by AIC and uses it for both the projection’s controls and the bootstrap VAR:

using Random: Xoshiro
rzc = dropmissing(rz, [:newsy, :y, :g])
sel = lagselect(rzc, [:newsy, :y, :g]; maxlags = 10, criterion = :aic)
nlags(sel)
9

AIC picks 9 lags (BIC would pick 2). Both the four lags of the Ramey–Zubairy specification and the AIC order are shown below. @formula takes a literal lag count, so each order is written out:

boot(f, p) = varbootstrap(lp(f, rzc; horizon = 20), rzc; vars = [:newsy, :y, :g],
                          nlags = p, nboot = 1000, rng = Xoshiro(20260916))
# the same seed gives both responses the same artificial samples
b4 = (y = boot(@formula(leads(y) ~ newsy + lags(newsy, 4) + lags(y, 4) + lags(g, 4)), 4),
      g = boot(@formula(leads(g) ~ newsy + lags(newsy, 4) + lags(y, 4) + lags(g, 4)), 4))
b9 = (y = boot(@formula(leads(y) ~ newsy + lags(newsy, 9) + lags(y, 9) + lags(g, 9)), 9),
      g = boot(@formula(leads(g) ~ newsy + lags(newsy, 9) + lags(y, 9) + lags(g, 9)), 9))
summarize(b4.y; level = 0.90)       # bias-corrected path, Hall percentile-t band

With four lags, GDP peaks at 0.30 after 10 quarters and spending at 0.38 after 11. On this long sample the bias correction moves the paths by at most 0.023. The 90% band excludes zero at horizons 0–13 and 18 for GDP and 1–17 for spending. With 9 lags the shape is the same (peaks of 0.29 for GDP at 11 quarters and 0.39 for spending at 11), but the bands are wider at long horizons and right-skewed: they exclude zero at horizons 0–19 for GDP and 1–16 for spending. Bands are pointwise, and because the percentile-\(t\) construction corrects for bias they need not be centred on the estimate. 0 of the 4000 draws fail, and the Pope correction applies in full in all four bootstraps.
